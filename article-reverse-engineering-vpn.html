Reverse-Engineering an Enterprise VPN Client

VPN clients run with the highest privileges on the box and speak protocols nobody documents. That makes them one of the most rewarding — and most overlooked — targets in a security review.

Research  ·  By Stephan Botes  ·  ~8 min read

Why VPN clients are worth the effort

A corporate VPN client is a near-perfect target. It runs as root or SYSTEM, it handles authentication material, it opens raw network interfaces, and it usually speaks a proprietary handshake the vendor never documented. It is installed on every remote endpoint and trusted by default. Break the client and you often break the trust boundary the entire remote-access model depends on — which is exactly why it deserves scrutiny before it ships to a fleet, not after.

The methodology: static, then dynamic

Two passes. The first reads the binary at rest; the second watches it run.

  • Static triage. Symbols, strings, and a disassembler such as Ghidra to map the binary — locate the crypto, the config parser, and any privileged helper process.
  • Dynamic observation. Run it in an isolated lab, capture the traffic, hook the interesting functions, and compare what actually crosses the wire against what the datasheet claims.

The fastest way to start is almost embarrassingly simple — ask the binary what it leaks about itself:

bash
# triage: what does the client tell you for free?
strings -n 8 vpnclient | grep -Ei 'password|token|secret|BEGIN.*KEY|http://|verify=0'

A single hardcoded http:// update URL, a verify=0 flag, or a private key baked into the binary is often the whole finding — discovered before you have written a line of exploit code.

Reading the handshake

Two questions drive any VPN review: how does the client authenticate, and does it verify the server it is talking to? The interesting failures cluster in predictable places:

  • TLS with certificate validation disabled, or pinned to a wildcard that any sibling host can present.
  • 802.1X / EAP flows that trust the network segment before the encrypted tunnel is actually established.
  • Credentials or session tokens cached in world-readable files or predictable temp paths.

Each of these turns a "secure" remote-access product into a man-in-the-middle or credential-theft opportunity — none of which is visible from the marketing page.

The privilege boundary is where the bugs live

Most clients split a low-privileged GUI from a high-privileged background service, talking to each other over a local socket or named pipe. If that service trusts anything the GUI sends without validating who is sending it, a normal local user becomes root or SYSTEM. That IPC boundary — not the crypto — is where the highest-impact bugs usually hide, because it is the part the vendor tested least.

Rebuilding it in the open

The most rigorous way to prove you understand a protocol is to reimplement it from observation. That is why I built an open-source client for the H3C SSL VPN — a Qt6/KF6 front end with full 802.1X support — reconstructed from the observed handshake rather than the vendor's code. A clean reimplementation does three useful things: it documents an undocumented protocol, it removes an opaque privileged binary from the trust chain, and it gives defenders something they can actually read and audit instead of trusting on faith.

Disclose responsibly

Findings against a shipping product go to the vendor first, with a reproducible proof of concept and a reasonable timeline before anything is published. Research is only worth doing if it makes the ecosystem safer — not if it burns the organizations that depend on the product while they are still waiting on a fix. Coordinated disclosure is the difference between security research and something else entirely.

What procurement should take from this

If you are about to deploy an appliance or a client that will run privileged on every endpoint, someone should tear it down before you trust it — not after the incident. That is precisely what a vendor product security review delivers: a go/no-go decision grounded in how the thing actually behaves under a hostile eye, backed by protocol and binary analysis, rather than a datasheet and a logo. The cost of the review is always smaller than the cost of the breach it prevents.
