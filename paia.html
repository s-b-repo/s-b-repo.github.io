PAIA Manual.

The section 51 manual every South African private body has been required to keep since the exemption lapsed at the end of 2021. What we hold, how to ask for it, and when we may say no.

Last updated 3 August 2026

This is the manual required of a private body by section 51 of the Promotion of Access to Information Act 2 of 2000 ("PAIA"). It describes the records Cyber Sec holds and how to request access to them.

Private bodies were exempted from having a section 51 manual for many years. That exemption lapsed on 31 December 2021, and since then every private body has been required to have one available. Most South African businesses still do not.

6. How to request access

  1. Complete the prescribed form for a request to a private body, as published in the PAIA Regulations, and available from inforegulator.org.za.
  2. Provide enough detail to identify the record and to identify you, plus the form of access you want and a contact address.
  3. State the right you are seeking to exercise or protect, and explain why the record is required to exercise or protect it. A request that does not do this must be refused — section 50(1)(a) allows access only where the record is required for the exercise or protection of a right.
  4. Send it to stephanbotesIT@proton.me.
  5. We will decide within 30 days and tell you in writing. That period may be extended by a further 30 days where the request covers a large number of records or requires a search through records held elsewhere.
  6. If a record contains third-party information, we must notify that party and give them the opportunity to make representations before we decide.

Fees

PAIA provides for a request fee and an access fee, both prescribed by regulation and revised from time to time. Rather than quote figures that may go out of date, we will tell you the current prescribed amounts in writing when you make a request. We may require a deposit where preparation is estimated to exceed six hours, and we will not process the request until any required fee is paid. The fee schedule published by the Information Regulator prevails over anything stated here.

7. Grounds on which access may be refused

Chapter 4 of Part 3 of PAIA obliges or permits us to refuse access in defined circumstances. The grounds most likely to apply here are:

  • Section 63 — unreasonable disclosure of personal information about a third party;
  • Section 64 — commercial information of a third party: trade secrets, financial or commercial information that could harm them, or information supplied in confidence;
  • Section 65 — breach of a duty of confidence owed to a third party;
  • Section 66 — information that could endanger the life or physical safety of an individual, or prejudice the security of property or of a system for protecting it;
  • Section 67 — privileged records;
  • Section 68 — our own commercial information;
  • Section 69 — research information; and
  • Section 70 — the public-interest override, which can compel disclosure despite the grounds above.

A note specific to our work. Records of client engagements describe live weaknesses in third-party systems. Disclosing them could reasonably be expected to prejudice the security of property and of the systems protecting it, engaging section 66, as well as section 65 and our contractual duties of confidence. Requests for that material will be refused save in exceptional circumstances, and we will notify the affected client of any such request.

8. If we refuse

We will give written reasons and tell you what you can do next. There is no internal appeal against a decision of a private body. You may:

  • lodge a complaint with the Information Regulator under section 77A within 180 days; or
  • apply to a court under section 78 within 180 days of being notified.
