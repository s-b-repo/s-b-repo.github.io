State-Sponsored Threat Actor Patterns

How nation-state operators differ from commodity criminals — initial access, living off the land, persistence, and what it means for your defenses.

Advisory  ·  By Stephan Botes  ·  ~6 min read

Not your average attacker

Most intrusions are opportunistic. A criminal crew wants money, wants it quickly, and will move on to a softer target the moment you stop being convenient. State-sponsored operators are a different animal entirely. They are funded, patient, and pointed at an objective that has nothing to do with a quick payout — espionage, positioning for future disruption, or long-term access to something they consider strategically valuable.

That difference in motive changes everything about how they behave. A ransomware affiliate is loud because speed matters more than stealth. A nation-state operator is quiet because being there next year matters more than being there today. If you defend against the former and assume the latter looks the same, you will miss them completely.

Initial access: precise, not opportunistic

Commodity attackers spray. Nation-state operators aim. The entry vectors we see attributed to this class of actor are deliberate and researched:

  • Targeted spear-phishing — lures crafted for a specific person, referencing real projects, real colleagues, and real business context rather than a generic "invoice attached".
  • Edge and appliance exploitation — the internet-facing VPN, firewall, or file-transfer box, because it is exposed, hard to audit, and often patched last.
  • Trusted-relationship and supply-chain abuse — compromising a vendor, a managed provider, or a software update channel so the victim opens the door to a trusted source themselves.

The common thread is that they would rather spend weeks finding the one right door than kick a hundred wrong ones. That patience at the front of the intrusion is your first tell.

Living off the land

The signature move of a capable state-sponsored operator is that they bring almost no malware. Once inside, they use what is already there — the legitimate administrative tooling, the signed binaries, the scripting engines your own admins rely on every day. This is "living off the land", and it is deliberately chosen to defeat detection that looks for malicious files.

When the intrusion runs on LOLBins and built-in admin utilities, there is no malicious executable to flag, no unusual binary to hash, no obvious payload on disk. The activity blends into the noise of normal administration. If your detection strategy is fundamentally "find the bad file", an operator who never drops one walks straight past it.

Persistence and stealth

Rather than plant a noisy backdoor, these actors prefer to become indistinguishable from a legitimate user. The persistence mechanism of choice is a valid account — one they stole, one they created, or one they quietly elevated — because a real credential raises no alarms and survives most incident cleanups.

From there they blend in: activity during business hours, from expected systems, using expected tools. Dwell time is measured in months, not days. We have investigated environments where an operator had legitimate credentials and simply lived in the network, and the only reason anyone noticed was an unrelated audit. The absence of an alarm is not the absence of an intruder.

Exfiltration: slow and quiet

A smash-and-grab crew rips data out as fast as the pipe allows and accepts the noise. A nation-state operator does the opposite. They stage data internally first — collecting, compressing, and stashing it in a location that looks unremarkable — and only then move it out gradually over encrypted channels that resemble ordinary traffic.

The goal is to keep the exfiltration under the threshold of anything that would trigger a review. Small transfers, normal-looking destinations, encryption everywhere. By the time a volume spike would have been obvious, the data is already gone in pieces nobody flagged.

Defensive implications

You do not beat this class of adversary with a better antivirus signature. You beat them by changing your assumptions. The posture that actually works:

  • Assume breach. Design and operate as if an operator is already inside with a valid credential, because that is the scenario they engineer for.
  • Engineer detection mapped to MITRE ATT&CK. Hunt for behaviours — anomalous use of admin tooling, unexpected account activity, staging — not just files. Map your coverage to the techniques these actors actually use and find the gaps.
  • Run threat-informed testing. Have someone authorised emulate these specific tradecraft patterns against your environment and tell you honestly what got caught and what did not.
  • Watch identity and the edge hardest. Those are the two places these operators live — the login they abuse and the appliance they enter through. Instrument them accordingly.

The difference between an organisation that detects a state-sponsored intrusion and one that reads about it later is rarely budget. It is whether the defence was built around how these actors actually operate, and validated under pressure rather than assumed on paper.
